The idea that Christians might wage a “holy war” has appeared in history, especially in the medieval crusades, but Catholic doctrine does not recognise a war that is holy in itself. The Church’s moral teaching distinguishes a legitimate, restrained use of force (the just‑war tradition) from the notion of a war that is sanctified by religion and therefore exempt from ordinary moral limits. 

A historical use of the term

In the Middle Ages the popes authorized the Crusades to recover the Holy Land and to combat heresy. In doing so they “instigated wars … and conferred spiritual benefits (e.g., indulgences) that only they could give”1. Some writers of the period even spoke of a “holy war” as a war “directly undertaken by the Popes for a religious purpose”1, and the Crusades were presented as a Christian response to the “infidel” Moors2. This language, however, was a loose or improper use of the term, not a doctrinal definition. 

The theological critique: holy war is impossible

Charles Journet argued that a war cannot be holy in the proper sense because the Church, by virtue of its canonical power, cannot wield violence. He wrote that “there has never yet been a holy war” and that “if holy war is taken in an attenuated sense … the popes … did in fact engage in warfare of this kind, but it was never a war of Christianity against Islam”3. Journet concluded that “the Church cannot engage in violence by virtue of its canonical power as successor of St Peter”1, and therefore a true “holy war” is a contradiction in terms. 

Modern magisterial teaching rejects a holy war

The Catechism states that “because of the evils and injustices that all war brings … we must do everything reasonably possible to avoid it”4, and the Compendium of the Social Doctrine of the Church declares that “a war of aggression is intrinsically immoral” and limits the use of force to strict conditions of self‑defence5. The Bishops of the Second Vatican Council likewise taught that governments may defend the common good, but only within the framework of just‑war theory, which seeks to limit both the outbreak of war and the way it is waged6. 

Holy war versus just war

James Turner Johnson’s typology places “crusade or holy war” at the extreme opposite of pacifism, describing it as a war that “admits unlimited force in the service of its transcendent cause”7. By contrast, the just‑war tradition requires a legitimate authority, a just cause, right intention, proportionality and last‑resort criteria, thereby rejecting the unlimited violence implied by a holy war. Contemporary Church documents echo this rejection: the Compendium calls violence “never a proper response” and stresses that even self‑defence must respect proportionality8. 

The Church’s current stance

Recent papal statements and the Dicastery for Culture and Education’s joint declaration note that “the Popes have condemned acts of violence, oppression, social injustice and slavery” and that many faithful have given their lives defending human dignity9. The United States Conference of Catholic Bishops emphasizes that Catholics must work to avoid war, promote peace, and that any use of force must be “discriminate, proportionate and a last resort”10. Thus the contemporary magisterium presents the Church as a minister of peace, not as a sponsor of wars declared holy. 

Conclusion

While the phrase “holy war” was employed in medieval rhetoric to describe the Crusades, Catholic theology has never taught that war can be sanctified by the Church. The Church affirms the just‑war tradition, which permits the use of force only under very strict conditions of legitimate authority, just cause and right intention, and it consistently condemns the idea of a war that is holy in itself. In today’s Church, the proper Christian response to conflict is the pursuit of peace, the protection of human dignity, and the careful, moral use of force only when absolutely necessary.
